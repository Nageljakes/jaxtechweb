The problem it was built for

Nobody loses a deal on price alone.

They lose it to the twenty minutes it took to reply, the note nobody logged, the photo that never got sent, and the follow-up that lived in someone’s head instead of the diary. A salesperson’s day is mostly admin wearing a sales badge.

The Dealership OS takes that layer away. Not by giving the floor another dashboard to fill in, but by doing the work itself — accepting the lead, opening the conversation, writing the notes, scoring the intent and keeping the diary straight while the human does the part only a human can do.

The system

Five things it does, all day, unattended.

Each of these runs on its own. Together they are the difference between a CRM that stores what happened and a system that makes it happen.

01 — Speed to lead

Answered before the tab closes

Show, don’t tell

One lead, from arrival to diary entry.

Lead in

+ seconds

Reply

Handover

Under the hood

A multi-bot architecture, not one overloaded assistant.

One bot doing everything is a single point of failure and a queue behind a lock. The Dealership OS splits the work across specialised agents that each own a narrow job and hand off cleanly — which is what makes the whole thing safe to leave running.

intake

Watches the CRM, accepts inbound leads and claims them so two agents never work the same enquiry.

messaging

Owns the WhatsApp side: greetings, vehicle photos, replies and delivery state.

monitor-bridge

Passively indexes live conversation into the customer record, without sitting in the message path.

inventory

Keeps the local stock and gallery cache warm and consistent with the group’s inventory.

scoring & notes

Reads the conversation for intent, writes the note and the lead likelihood score back to the CRM.

lifecycle

Holds the database locks, moves leads between states and cleans up after anything that fails midway.

Where the line sits: the system opens conversations, documents them and keeps the diary honest. It does not quote deals, commit to prices or close on a customer’s behalf — anything with money or a promise attached waits for a person, by design rather than as a setting somebody has to find.
