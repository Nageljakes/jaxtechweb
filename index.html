    Context & History

    The person behind Tiny

    Two years, one obsession: teach a machine to actually get things done.

    Jaxtech is built and run by Jakes Nagel, based in Gauteng, South Africa.

    By day, Jakes works in vehicle finance and dealership operations — the kind of world with no shortage of forms, compliance checks and repetitive admin. That's a good vantage point for spotting exactly what software should be doing instead of a person.

    Tiny is the result of two years spent building toward that, mostly nights and weekends. When AI first started making headlines, well before "agentic AI" was a term anyone used, Jakes was already convinced it would end up doing real work, not just answering questions. Rather than wait for the tools to mature, he went down to the foundations — how models actually work, what they can and can't do — and used that to track the AI transformation as it happened, month by month.

    That groundwork became Chappie, the first project: getting large AI models to run locally, on hardware never really built for the job. It was slow, unforgiving, and taught every hard lesson about inference, memory and reliability the hard way. Tiny is what came after — the same idea, rebuilt as an agentic harness that plans, uses tools and finishes a task, running on online-based inferencing instead of a struggling local GPU. It's the assistant you're talking to above.

    Questions

    The things everyone asks.

    What actually is Tiny?

    Tiny is an AI agent, not a chatbot. The difference matters: a chatbot answers you, an agent does the work. Tiny plans a task out into steps, uses real tools to carry them out — reading files, running code, calling other systems — and keeps going until the job is finished. The chat on this page is a small demo of it. A custom agent built for your workflow does considerably more.

    What kind of task is actually worth handing over?

    The best candidates are repetitive, rule-shaped and eat time every week: pulling numbers into the same report, chasing the same follow-ups, re-typing information from one system into another, checking documents against a checklist. If you can explain the job to a new hire in ten minutes, it's usually a good fit. One-off creative judgement calls are usually not.

    Where does the AI run, and who can see my data?

    Two different answers, so it's worth being precise. The chat on this page runs on hosted cloud inference — your message is sent to an AI provider to generate Tiny's reply, and if you submit an enquiry I read that conversation myself to follow up. An agent built for you can be deployed either way: on cloud inference, or on hardware on your own premises where the data never leaves the building. Which one is right depends on how sensitive the work is, and it's one of the first things we'd decide together.

    What does a custom agent cost?

    It depends on how many systems the agent has to touch and how much judgement the task needs, so I scope each one before quoting. You get a written scope and a fixed number before any work starts — no hourly creep. Running costs are separate and stay yours: AI model usage, any paid APIs, and hosting if it runs in the cloud. I'll estimate those honestly upfront, including where they might grow.

    How long does it take to build?

    A tightly-scoped single-workflow agent is usually a couple of weeks from agreed scope to something you're using. Anything touching several systems, or needing access approvals from a third party, takes longer — mostly waiting on the access, not the building. I'd rather quote a realistic date than a hopeful one.

    What if my task turns out not to be automatable?

    Then I'll tell you that, before you spend money. Some tasks look automatable but depend on judgement, messy exceptions or a system that simply won't let anything in. Finding that out during scoping costs you a conversation. Finding it out halfway through a build costs a great deal more, so I'd rather be blunt early.

    Who am I actually dealing with?

    Me — Jakes Nagel, in Gauteng. Jaxtech is a one-person operation, which I think is worth being upfront about. It means you deal directly with the person building the thing, with nothing lost in translation between a salesperson and a developer. It also means I'm honest about capacity: if a project is bigger than I can do properly, I'll say so rather than take it on and disappoint you.

    Do you still do smart-home and property automation?

    Yes — it's one of the things an agent can be pointed at, and it's where a lot of the early work happened. Energy monitoring, geyser scheduling, irrigation and camera automation are all real projects, usually running on a controller on the property so they keep working when the power and the fibre don't. It's one kind of project rather than a separate business.
